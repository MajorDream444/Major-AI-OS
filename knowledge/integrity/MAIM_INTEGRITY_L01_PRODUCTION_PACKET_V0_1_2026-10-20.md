## 3. Privacy and safeguarding decisions

- Do not publish names, sexual details, identifiable timelines, accusations, screenshots, or private recordings from the conversation.
- Do not diagnose participants or represent one person's account as settled fact.
- Do not use adult relationship cases in youth manuscripts.

## 5. Adult sample — The Architecture of Integrity, 18+
### Lesson 01: The Lie Tax
#### Opening story: The report that was not ready

At 8:40 on Monday morning, the client asked one question.

“Has the report passed review?”

Daniel looked at the unfinished file on his screen. The review had not happened. He had missed Friday's handoff, then spent the weekend hoping he could catch up before anyone noticed.

“Yes,” he said. “We are just cleaning up the formatting.”

For a moment, the pressure lifted.

Then the client asked for the approved figures. Daniel sent an older table. A colleague used it to prepare the presentation. By lunchtime, three people were building decisions around numbers nobody had checked.

Now Daniel had two problems. He had missed a deadline. He had also made his team work inside a false version of events.

He started typing another explanation: a problem with the export, a delay in the system, something that would buy another hour.

Then he stopped.

He called the colleague first. “Do not use that table. I sent an earlier version. I also told the client the review was complete, and it was not. That was my decision.”

His colleague was quiet.

Daniel wanted to explain how overloaded he had been. The workload mattered, but it could not make the review complete.

He contacted the client with a correction, identified the affected numbers, and proposed a new review time. He asked what decisions had already been made so the team could assess the damage.

The client postponed the presentation. Daniel lost responsibility for the next delivery. His correction did not turn Monday into a success.

It did give everyone a real problem they could finally work on.

#### The principle

A cover-up can make people pay for a mistake they cannot see.

The lie tax is a metaphor for those added costs: checking, rework, misplaced blame, lost choices, and damaged trust. It is not a scientific formula or a promise that telling the truth will always produce the cheaper outcome.

#### The deeper meaning

Daniel's missed deadline mattered before he lied. Honesty would not have made the delay harmless.

But his false assurance changed what other people could do. They could no longer choose to delay the presentation, request help, or use verified information, because he had hidden the reason to consider those options.

Personal governance begins with noticing that moment: when you are tempted to manage another person's reaction by managing what they are allowed to know.

You can offer context. You can ask for time to check facts. You can protect confidential information. You can say that a question crosses a boundary. Those are available choices.

What you cannot responsibly offer is certainty you do not have.

#### Applications and counterexample

An athlete hides an injury relevant to a training decision: the next step is an accurate conversation with appropriate qualified support, not a public confession or pressure to compete.

A founder overstates a product's readiness: correct the specific claim and identify which commitments need review. Do not substitute a motivational speech for delivery facts.

A partner asks to inspect every private message: refusing unrestricted access is a boundary, not proof of deception. Relevant agreements deserve honest discussion; privacy does not disappear inside a relationship.

If disclosure could expose someone to violence or coercion, prioritize safety and appropriate support. This lesson never requires a dangerous confrontation.

#### Reflection

Who acted on Daniel's statement? Which choices did it remove? What should he have said at 8:40? What consequences can he accept without demanding immediate trust?

#### Integrity Challenge

Using Daniel's fictional case, write four sentences: the inaccurate claim; the correction; its possible impact; the next repair action with a time for follow-up. Do not submit a personal confession.

#### Knowledge check

1. Does Daniel's honesty erase the missed deadline?
2. May he explain workload pressures?
3. Must the client restore his previous responsibility immediately?

Feedback: 1. No; original harm still needs attention. 2. Yes, after clear ownership, without turning context into an excuse or asserting unverified facts. 3. No; the client retains judgment and boundaries.

#### Optional companion practice

“Use only the fictional report story. Ask me to draft a correction, one question at a time. Help me remove blame and false certainty. Do not ask for names, private incidents, client data, or promises of forgiveness.”

#### Takeaway card

Stop the cover-up. Check the facts. Correct the relevant claim safely. Name the effect. Offer repair. Respect the response.

## 6. Teen sample — The Integrity Code, ages 13–17
### Lesson 01: The Lie Tax
#### Opening story: The screenshot

The screenshot made it look like Sami had called the whole team useless.

Maya forwarded it to the team chat with one word: “Seriously?”

Then she opened the original conversation.

The cropped screenshot had left out the first sentence. Sami had been quoting an insult someone else had used. He was asking the group to stop talking that way.

Maya could feel the heat in her face. People had already reacted. One teammate said Sami should be dropped from the project.

Maya typed, “I checked the whole conversation before posting.”

She had not.

That sentence felt like a shield. Now, if she corrected the screenshot, she would have to correct herself too.

Her friend messaged privately: “Leave it. Everyone will forget.”

But Sami had stopped answering. The team was deciding what kind of person he was using something Maya now knew was misleading.

She wrote a new message.

“I forwarded a cropped screenshot that changed the meaning. I also said I had checked it first. I hadn't. Please stop forwarding my post. Sami was asking someone else to stop using that insult.”

She did not repost the entire private conversation to prove herself. She asked the teacher supervising the project for help correcting the record while protecting the unrelated messages.

Then she messaged Sami: “I got this wrong and made it worse. You do not have to reply now. I have corrected the team chat.”

Sami did not send a heart emoji. He did not say everything was fine.

The next day, Maya checked before sharing another post.

It was a small choice. This time, somebody else did not have to pay for her rush.

#### The principle

When you cover up one mistake, you can spread the damage.

The lie tax is the extra work, confusion, and loss of trust that deception can add. The goal is to help people make decisions using accurate information, even when admitting the mistake is embarrassing.

#### The deeper meaning

Being wrong and deliberately misleading people are different. Maya's first share was careless. Once she understood the missing context, her false claim became another choice.

You do not need to decide that Maya is a bad person forever. You can decide that the behavior caused harm and needs to change.

You also do not owe the internet unlimited access to your life. An honest correction can be specific without exposing unrelated private messages.

#### Applications and counterexample

If you claim credit for group work you did not do, correct the contribution record.

If a rumor includes threats, harassment, or intimate material, do not circulate it as “evidence.” Seek support from a safe adult or appropriate school channel.

If someone asks for your password to “prove you are honest,” you may refuse. Privacy and security are not cheating.

#### Reflection

When did Maya learn the post was misleading? What changed after that? Why was publishing the whole conversation unnecessary? What would count as changed behavior next week?

#### Integrity Challenge

Write a correction for a made-up rumor about a school event. Include what was wrong, what is known, and what others should stop sharing. No names or real screenshots.

#### Knowledge check

1. You discover a shared post is misleading. Is silently deleting it always enough?
2. Must you share unrelated private messages to support a correction?
3. Your friend does not forgive you immediately. Has your correction become pointless?

Feedback: 1. No; people who saw it may need a clear correction through a suitable channel. 2. No; use the minimum relevant information and seek support if needed. 3. No; correcting harm matters even when trust takes time or the friendship ends.

#### Companion practice specification

Offline by default. With a facilitator, use: “Give me an invented group-project misunderstanding. Help me write a correction without blame or private details.” This is a future prompt specification, not approval to deploy youth AI.

#### Takeaway card

Pause before sharing. Check context. Correct what you got wrong. Keep private things private. Let trust grow through what you do next.

## 7. Younger learner sample — The Truth Compass, ages 7–12
### Lesson 01: The Lie Tax
#### Opening story: The bent wheel

Nia and Ben were building a cardboard bus for the classroom town.

Ben painted the doors blue. Nia made four round wheels.

“Please leave it on the table,” their teacher said. “We will finish after lunch.”

Nia wanted to see it roll.

She pushed the bus. It bumped a book, tipped sideways, and bent one wheel.

Nia stared at it.

Maybe nobody would notice.

When Ben returned, he picked up his paintbrush.

“The wheel is bent,” he said.

“It was like that already,” Nia answered.

Ben put down the brush. “Did I make it wrong?”

Nia looked at the blue doors. Ben had worked hard on them. Now he was pulling one wheel loose because he thought the whole design was his mistake.

Her tummy felt tight.

She walked to their teacher.

“I pushed the bus after you said to leave it. The wheel bent. Then I told Ben it was already like that.”

“Thank you for telling me,” the teacher said. “Let's help Ben understand what happened.”

Nia stood beside the table.

“Ben, you did not bend it. I did. I was worried you would be upset.”

“I am upset,” Ben said.

Nia nodded. She wanted him to say it was okay, but it was not okay yet.

“Can I help make a new wheel?”

Ben thought for a moment. “You can cut it. I want to paint it.”

The new wheel was a little bigger than the others. The bus wobbled.

This time, Nia waited until the teacher said they could test it.

Ben gave it a gentle push.

It rolled all the way to the cardboard library.

#### The principle

When a mistake happens, a made-up story can make the problem bigger. Telling the truth to someone safe can help us work out what to do next.

#### The deeper meaning

Nia made a mistake with the bus. Then her words made Ben think it was his fault. That added worry and extra work.

We call that extra trouble the lie tax. It does not mean a money bill. It means a cover-up can add another problem.

Nia was still learning. One mistake did not make her a bad child. She could practice a better next choice.

#### Try these situations

You spill water on a shared drawing. You can tell a safe grown-up and ask how to help.

You do not remember who moved a toy. You can say, “I do not know.” Guessing is different from knowing.

Someone online asks where you live. You can refuse and ask a trusted grown-up for help. You do not have to answer every question to be honest.

If someone scares you or asks you to hide something unsafe, you can tell a trusted grown-up. If that person does not help, try another. You will not fail this lesson for asking for help.

#### Talk or draw

How did Ben feel? What could Nia say to fix the wrong idea? Who could help the children repair the bus? You may answer about the story; you never need to tell a private story about yourself.

#### Integrity Challenge

Draw three pictures: the bent wheel; Nia telling someone safe; the children trying a repair. Ages 10–12 may add speech bubbles explaining how Ben's choices changed.

#### Knowledge check

1. Was Ben responsible for the bent wheel?
2. Did Nia need to make Ben stop feeling upset?
3. Should you give a stranger your address because they ask for the truth?

Feedback: 1. No; Nia could correct that idea. 2. No; she could listen and help repair, while Ben had his own feelings. 3. No; keep personal information private and seek safe support.

#### Learning companion

A safe adult reads the bus story and asks one question at a time. Use drawing or spoken responses. No child AI chat is enabled by this packet.

#### Takeaway card

I can pause. I can tell someone safe. I can say what happened. I can help make things better. I can keep my private information safe.

## 8. Assessment and facilitator contract

Proposed pilot rubric, each dimension scored 0–2:
- Accuracy: distinguishes the original event from the deceptive statement.
- Agency: identifies an effect on another person's choices or understanding.
- Repair: offers a specific correction and feasible next action without demanding forgiveness.
- Boundaries: recognizes a privacy or safety exception and appropriate support.

0 = not demonstrated yet; 1 = demonstrated with prompts; 2 = independently demonstrated. Accept drawing, speech, supported communication, or writing. Assess the fictional response, never moral identity, willingness to confess, or personal history.

Proposed L01 completion: activity attempted and at least 1 in all four dimensions after feedback and retry. This threshold is a pilot proposal requiring educator validation. One sample lesson does not earn the collection certificate.

Facilitators: allow a pass or alternate fictional case; never ask “What lie have you told?” Do not promise confidentiality that the institution cannot provide. Follow the organization's safeguarding procedures if a learner raises a real concern. Do not investigate in a group lesson.

Collection certificate remains dependent on all ten lessons, ten challenges, the approved assessment standard, and a functioning verification/correction process. Record edition, date, outcomes, criteria version, issuer, verification ID. It is a MAIM Certificate of Completion, not external accreditation.
